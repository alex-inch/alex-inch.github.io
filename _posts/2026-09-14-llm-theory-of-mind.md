*Expanding from a comment on HackerNews.*
<!-- LLMs like ChatGPT or Claude may be capable of solving incredibly challenging maths problems or maybe killing everyone (or maybe not...), but they fail in a really interesting way to me. They cannot empathise with someone other than the person they're talking to. -->

<!-- I mean that in the contexts that I use LLMs in. Chiefly where I see this is in writing, or producing charts. These are both disciplines where you need to be capable of *empathy* --- with the person reading your text or viewing your chart. As my editor likes to say, you need to do the thinking for them, making it as easy as possible to understand what you're presenting. -->

<!-- When you convey information to someone else, a lot of what matters is the judgement: is this relevant? Do the need to know? Do they have the necessary context to understand this? And, at the end of the day, do they care? -->

<!-- But LLMs can't do it. I've been entertaining this thought for a while, but it was recently spurred by reading [this blog post](https://www.serpentine.com/posts/2026/x3-stripes/) which is clearly heavily co-authored with Claude. Take a look at the plot. -->


<!-- The way LLMs produce charts is so interesting, because it's a great example of a terribly basic failing that - the models have barely any empathy for a 3rd party reader. So if you use one to write or make vis, the output gets overloaded with minutiae that came up during the conversation, even if it would overwhelm a reader coming fresh. -->

<!-- Take this chart that I saw recently in a presumably AI-generated article. The x-axis label of the first plot mentions that it shows gridlines every 8 ticks. But why? Who does that? I don't think I've ever seen a person do that. Only an LLM that's been told by the user to add gridlines every 8 ticks, and wants to impress upon its user that it has done it.

I see this kind of failing constantly when I experiment with LLMs. When collaborating on an article, I'll give it feedback, and the next iteration will address my comment so in such a direct, cackhanded manner that it's worse than before any fix was attempted at all. When an LLM writes code, it sprinkles the files with inane comments describing minor details that I've told it to sort out (like `# now uses the async interface when num_workers is 0`), rather than the kind of information that would be useful to a newcomer. --> 

To be fair to our imminent overlords or the Terminator or whatever's meant to be happening (with a very rational probability >10%), this isn't unprecented behaviour. I've also seen from fresh grads. When they write up research or present a project, they don't tell you about the finished product, they tell you what they did. If they worked hard on something, even if it was cut, you will hear about it. And you won't care. What happens is a failure to empathise with the audience, and filter out the irrelevant detail.2 

<!-- I encounter a similar thing when trying to work with an LLM to write an article - they cannot help including information or detail from the conversation, rather than empathising with the reader and filtering out what they might already know or not care about. --> 

<!-- LLMs may be capable of solving decades-old maths problems, but on this particular axis they seem to be floundering at the level of a fresh graduate that's desperate to talk about what they've done, rather than what their audience needs to know. -->
